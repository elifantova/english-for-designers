## Showing up anyway  

This is not about a perfect design, or a big success, but about what happens when things don’t quite go your way.

## It starts with a concept.

Last year, my friend Natálie Chocholová and I attended an open call to design a visual identity for FAMUFEST 41 — a student film festival with the theme Happy End. We built our idea around nostalgia. Film cassettes. Analogue textures. The kind of tactile, imperfect beauty that flickers in memory.


![Untitled-16_11zon](https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/10279f01-3589-44a6-a916-9d804a43042c)


We made it to the second round of the selection process. And then — we didn’t make it further.

## The project moved on without us.

Months passed. The new visual identity was released. I scrolled past it on Instagram — a little disappointed, maybe a little critical. You can’t help it, really.


![Untitled-16_11zon](https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/fb02e826-c07e-4a43-86b3-fdd2b4b77c78)


Then, out of the blue, a message arrived.

A friend of mine, who was helping organise the festival, told me the new design team was in a bind. They were behind on content. The timeline was tight. She asked me if I could help out — just with social media graphics. A few posts, maybe some editing.

## And I paused.

Not because I didn’t want to help — but because it was strange. Helping to bring someone else’s vision to life, especially when your own had been left behind. Especially when you don’t quite believe in the visual direction you’re now being asked to continue.

## I said yes.

At first, it was simple enough. Sizing assets, prepping posts. But as the festival approached, things began to shift. The original designers stepped back. Whether they were overwhelmed or just uninterested, they stopped showing up. And I found myself stepping in.

Suddenly, I wasn’t just supporting the visual identity — I was holding it up.

Creating, adjusting, adapting. On-site, in real time. Even though it wasn’t mine. Even though I wouldn’t have done it that way.

## But I did it anyway.

Because what I realised — and what I try to bring into every project now — is this: Design isn’t always about the idea you love the most. Sometimes, it’s about the commitment to finish something someone else started.

It’s about trust. It’s about being dependable.

## Principles of this story: 
### - Integrity over ego
I stepped in to help, even after being rejected — not out of pride, but out of professionalism, care for the project and care for the people organising it. Design is about solving problems, not just self-expression.

### - Reliability builds trust
I didn’t just do what I was originally asked to — I went beyond the brief, due to the absence of certain people. The most dependable designers are the ones people come back to.

### - Flexibility is a superpower
Even though the visual style wasn’t my own, I adapted to it and worked with what was at hand. Adaptability is key in design — not every project will reflect your personal style, but every project is an opportunity to contribute meaningfully.
  
### - Growth through adversity
I took a situation that could’ve felt bitter or demotivating and turned it into a learning experience. Even though it was not my dream job due to the visual style, I still enjoyed it very much. 

### - Design is about people
This isn’t just a story about graphics. It’s about people — my friend who reached out, the organisers who needed help, the audience who experienced the festival. At its core, design serves people.
